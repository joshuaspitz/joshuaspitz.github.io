About

I am completing my PhD at Leuphana University of Lüneburg on ant communities in a subtropical tree diversity experiment in SE China, studying ant trophic interactions, diversity, as well as compositional and functional shifts across levels of tree species richness. Fieldwork included extensive litter surveys and the identification of ground-foraging and cryptic litter ants.

Before that, I studied at TU Munich, where I worked on wild bee–plant interaction data along a land-use gradient in the Biodiversity Exploratories and on honeybee foraging along an urbanization gradient.

I am interested in combining classical taxonomic expertise with novel techniques to elucidate the mechanisms behind changes in insect communities and their functions. I analyze data in R.

More of my insect photography, with identifications checked by the community, is on iNaturalist ↗

Three layers of data

My work builds up from who is there, to what they are like, to what they do.

Layer 1 · Taxonomy

Who is there

My research rests on classical taxonomic data: ants sampled in the field, sorted and identified to species level under the microscope. Focus-stacked images come close to the view under the microscope and show the characters that identification relies on.

Layer 2 · Traits

What they are like

Measuring morphological traits on identified specimens adds a functional layer. Traits link species to how they forage, what they eat and how they move through their habitat, and they reveal shifts in communities that species counts alone can miss.

→ Related: Spitz et al. (2026), preprint

    Trait interpretation following Hoenle et al. (2023).

    Layer 3 · Interactions & functions

    What they do

    Baiting surveys capture ant behavior in the field: how quickly baits are removed, a direct measure of an ecosystem function, and which species use which resources, analyzed as interaction networks.
